## **A trip to say goodbye**

I finally went back to China in Spring 2025, after a long interval of 7,5 years. Moving between European countries and cities, COVID and the lockdowns, old and new jobs, as well as my escapism from politics in the home country and family dramas, resulted in this prolonged detachment. For a country alternating between boasting about «China speed» and cursing [«Lying flat» (<ruby>躺<rp>(</rp><rt>tǎng</rt><rp>)</rp>平<rp>(</rp><rt>píng</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>)](https://madeinchinajournal.com/2023/01/08/lying-flat-profiling-the-tangping-attitude/), 7,5 years can be enough for an official to start up, to become prominent, and collapse into corruption.

What has to be asked, after such a long time away from a home country, is not «why don't you go back», but «why do you»? My father's death gave me an excuse: not only to bury him, but also to bury some old memories, as well as to reveal the part of myself that scatters all over the state with memories and friendship.[^2] Hence, my article about burial, fermentation, and reconnection with memories.

## **Burying memories**

My father passed away in late 2024 and was cremated shortly afterwards. This sounds like a very important life change for almost everyone, but I had not spoken with him or seen his face since 2018. «Even the wisest mandarin cannot judge familial disputes» -- {% ruby 清官難斷家務事 qīngguān nánduàn jiāwùshì %}, as a Chinese proverb goes, so let me bury most of the past stories. For me, dealing with his death was not about my grief or the loss of a father since his absence was a given. Although the eventual burial of his presence and the trauma left in my life still need some processing.  Instead, I needed to go back for bureaucratic processes and for recollecting his ashes and memories in different cities.

This trip started in the town where I was born, Canton/Guangzhou: a city of a distinct Cantonese culture with an influx of economic immigrants in the 1980s–2000s, my parents being part of them. My father came to the city in 1990, together with many of his fellow Hunanese and Henanese. His name, Hengjun, still records his connection with the village he was born in at the foot of the South Great Mount Heng in Hunan; while his «personnel dossier» {% ruby 人事檔案 rénshì dàng'àn %} remains in his father's working space in an aluminium factory in Zhengzhou, Henan (see below), together with his first wife and my half-brother.[^3] After working in multiple factories together with his country-people, his skills as one of the earliest college students after the Cultural Revolution became recognised and soon, together with friends having grey-zone connections with Hong Kong capitals and French technologies, he started a small company producing hypodermic needles. After having hired a young accountant with whom he had a child (me), the company boomed for some years, until my father's catastrophic views on markets and slow reactions to the booming of Shenzhen led the company to collapse and go bankrupt in 1999. Guangzhou's gradual intensification of state-controlled companies over small businesses, together with my father's departure from his long-time collaborators (including my mother), made a comeback impossible for him. He found a new job teaching Marxism in colleges (and later universities), while starting to get more into gambling, antiquarian collections, unreliable investments, as well as inappropriate sexual approaches to his students. He died alone in a small apartment in an [urban village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_village_(China)) with debt.

I, of course, have almost no idea what he did in those years. After my parents' divorce, when I was still young, he appeared only during Chinese New Year or in the summer. The longest time we spent together was a short trip in 2010. As his first child built a family in Paris, he was more often in France during his free time, even after I came to Europe in 2017. Many times, I asked him if we could meet up in Brussels, Strasbourg, or Paris, with no response. My last two messages to him on my phone were «I will be in Paris for work next week, will you and my half-brother have time to meet up?» and a «Happy Birthday» in 2018, both of which received no response. To collect memories with him also means to trace all my attempts to be a nice child: after all, I thought I had tried my best.

Still, it was by retrieving his few belongings that I connected to the last part of his life, of which I had no knowledge. To take back those belongings still in the place where he died, alone, suddenly with no medical explanation, I needed to get to his rented dorm. Through his colleague, I only got in contact with a representative of his landlord, who refused to meet me because «your father's death brought misfortune to my apartment». No explanation; the landlord had already cleaned the house and only some documents were waiting to be checked, as well as some ceramics that his colleague claimed to originate from the 18th century and to be worth a six-digit sum. I thanked the colleague with perfect skills of performance, with an eye on those clearly fake ceramics. These were the only things my father left for me except for the debts. These and some questions—Why had he purchased them? Who had persuaded him that those ceramics would be worth so much? Why had he not thought of consulting his child, a professional on ancient ceramics, about them? What if he knew that those were all fake? But on the other hand, what if cleaning up the room of the dead brings more trauma or makes it possible to know my father better?

I didn't get to enter the place where he failed to wake up from a fatal sleep.

To bury the memory is to bury the trauma, and it’s better to open the wounds together with others who also endured the trauma, first and foremost, his ex-wives and children. After fetching my father’s ashes (as I will mention later in this story), we went to a small local noodle bistro with my mother, my father's other ex-wife (let’s call her Madam X), and her son, due to local tradition. The first time these two women in retirement met was at the time to fetch the ashes of their common ex-husband. We were very worried in advance about a potential conflict because of the violent ending of my father’s first marriage. But after a social greeting and a short period of social awkwardness, the long night started with Madam X’s words, «It was tough to have lived with him, right?» My mother responded, «Finally, we can say goodbye to that xxx (cursing).» It ended with a table of twelve bottles of beer by the two women, countless cheers, songs and cursings that could overturn the roof of the bistro till midnight, with the two children of the man listening to those jokes fermented from pain. We then decided not to keep a tomb for his ashes, but to «bury» him in the Yellow River: hence, no *lieu de mémoire* (to quote Pierre Nora) with a physical tomb (that is mentioned later in the text).

Having been relieved for months about this night, I now understand it with bitterness. As people who shall die later, we have the privilege to decide which kind of memories we want to keep and bury with regard to the dead person: as we say, we are «sealing a coffin with determined judgment» of them. Not joking about the concept of {% ruby 孝 xiào %} (unsatisfactorily translated as «filial piety»), I find it sad that a clearly negative memory of one person can be a fatal negation of one’s dignity. Yes, this man brought more trauma and harm than joy and healing in his family life, but he did not commit crimes as those known in history with notorious fame (no need to say that they should not be demonized either). At the same time, he was remembered by many as a good lecturer; many people in his circle remembered him as a nice friend or a trustworthy colleague. Do our different memories of this same person invalidate each other? Or do I have to acknowledge all the memories from different people? Do I really know my father before we bury memories about him, and did anyone do? 

Maybe, after the «burial», we should have left him a blank book (just like Empress Wu’s blank tomb stele), so that everyone having known him could write whatever they want about him.

**«Burying» the body**

For almost all of humankind, burial is an important ritual. How we deal with human remains, especially those of our relatives, reflects not only our understanding of human existence after biological death, but also what constitutes a human being in remembrance.

Funerals in China still maintain a special status, even in a society that generally keeps religion at a variable distance. Despite official atheism and the resulting simplification of traditional rituals, practices such as burning joss paper and prayers from all kinds of religious traditions, from Daoism to «imagined Western» Christianity, as well as Buddhist prayers in Sanskrit, Tibetan and Mandarin, remain popular, even among Communist Party officials. Younger generations can be [more creative in joss paper](https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1016941) or tomb forms, but for us millennials, funerals remain a strange mixture of local practices, untraceable rituals, as well as performances for a growingly recessing social circle of the dead. 

The tolerance of, or perhaps better put, the encouragement of the funerary industry is closely connected to economic development and the official stance of shifting the burden of welfare and caretaking onto private families.[^6] For local governments, funerary income is one of the few sources of revenue they can keep in their own pockets rather than having to send to the central government. They will therefore make every penny they can from the dead, while the social milieu continues to take death and ancestors seriously, at least among my father’s generation.

To generate a constant income, it’s common for local governments to promote the burial of funerary urns. Amid the obligatory cremation policy, this fits well with the traditional concept of “burial for rest” while also saving precious land, another resource local governments have at hand for generating revenue. In a city like Zhengzhou, a standard tomb for human ashes, taking space of no more than four square metres, costs € 3.500 (red packets and tips/bribes excluded). Even after death, my father can only rent his resting place for twenty years, since the state holds exclusive property rights to the land: an extension of this lease costs almost twice as much.[^7] Usually, the cemetery also provides obligatory services at a high price: standardised steles cost more than € 200, in addition to engraving, yearly cleaning service, etc. 

It is also mentally disturbing to actually be present in such a highly standardised cemetery, or «the city of the dead» (Nekropolis), as the Greeks put it. They are often built as a separate zone away from the city of the living, because burying the dead is also an official ritual meant to alienate them from the living society, both physically and mentally, and to (re)move them to another community that belongs to no living person. The uniform design evokes modern apartments, with even trees and bushes planted as if in a phalanx. It simply feels unsettling to stand in such a large cemetery for generations of people, or better yet, a carefully designed disposal centre for human beings alongside other ritual items (paper flowers and joss paper). Far away from our postmodern metropolis, the dead are designed to «live» just like the living, albeit with premodern rituals. Below them lay the ancestors since the last first century CE, in various and ever-evolving funerary styles and architectures. Those who send/remove their parents and grandparents to this cemetery may eventually join them on a higher level, until the Yellow River swallows the field and offers new spaces for more tombs. We are connected with our ancestors and unknown people in the past, horizontally in life and vertically in death.

Coming back to my father, after his death we were confronted with the dilemma of what we should do with his ashes. He believed in no sacred river or mountain where his body should be disposed of, nor was he a Buddhist and yet he still considered a dead body to be so important that he had arranged for the ashes of his father to take a round tour from his birthplace to his resting tomb across five Chinese provinces. To ask a state-doctrined materialist Marxist (who believed neither in spiritual reincarnation nor in an afterworld) how to deal with his ashes is perhaps as strange as to ask my vegetarian grandma how to cook meat: so there’s no way to confirm if our solution is respectful. If neither my half-brother nor I have the energy or emotion to take long-term care of this man’s ashes and further rituals (for instance, sweeping his tomb every year, burning joss paper and flowers on the death anniversary, etc.), then perhaps the physical presence of his ashes does not connect to our feelings. We ended up unintentionally adhering to a very Buddhist solution: to avoid «addiction to the flesh» 執着肉身, and especially to avoid the remembrance of the trauma, we decided to lay his ashes into the Yellow River. It is ironic for a Red Guard having attacked old traditions (including holding his father to struggle sessions) during the Cultural Revolution to get his ashes into the river with so much historical meaning. His ashes now lie either in the belly of the fish or on the river bank.

As he did not believe in a soul, may we wish ourselves peace with him.

## **Recollecting (hi)stories** 

I have to admit: I took an anthropological gaze during my trip in China. I intentionally chose to live in shared dorms for jobseekers in Guangzhou and Shanghai, ate in illegal or mini restaurants in urban villages, and took slow-speed trains for 21 hours across the country trying to observe people on trains or buses that I had taken for two decades since childhood. On the other hand, it was an experience that few foreigners would actually have since these long trains and shared dorms are spaces for students, jobseekers, budget travellers, as well as «losers in society». In Guangzhou, you may still have a chance to meet foreign merchants in mini restaurants (within specific districts) or interregional buses due to the reviving [«low-end globalisation»](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/W/bo27128812.html). They are mostly hidden behind the shining post-modern skyscrapers, high-tech buildings, landmarks (I slept in a 12-person dorm one street away from Beijing Road, the Hauptstraße in Guangzhou), historical blocks (if you visit Guangzhou, check the housing in the historical medical wholesale area to the north of the renowned Shamian Island: affordable and convenient), and easily get lost from large-scale descriptions, ethnological studies, or newspapers. 

For a flâneur who had almost forgotten his mother tongue after leaving his hometown for two decades and the country for more than seven years, this trip truly felt like a reconnection: a reconnection to an ever-changing space and society that I didn’t recognize on the first sight, but immersed into soon (this was, however, not the case in Beijing; but that'll be another article).

It was also a quick goodbye: in this Chinese megacity with a vivid underground dissident community and the largest African and South Asian diasporas, I witnessed the forced closure of an independent cinema during my short stay (below), police violence against Black residents who were granted visas for only days or weeks, and a growing recession around the city, despite the shining Canton Tower. Spaces that I knew were closed for long, bookstores bankrupted or became more commercialised, and small shops had no chance to compete with large brands with extremely cheap delivery services based on platform-level exploitation of [«delivery bros» 外賣小哥](https://www.suhrkamp.de/buch/hu-anyan-ich-fahr-pakete-aus-in-peking-t-9783518475157) (notwithstanding, with a connotation of [a gendered institutionalisation](https://madeinchinajournal.com/2026/01/12/gendered-organisation-of-platform-food-delivery-work-in-china/) in the platform food delivery work). Still, I see chances to rewrite and reexamine the rich (hi)stories of merchants, architectures, artefacts, cityscapes, river islands, etc. This city is in need of a true «People’s History».

It was after my writing project started that I visited the exhibition [«Mensch oder Objekt»](http://www.vkm-vpst.de/mensch-oder-objekt/) in Völkerkundemuseum Heidelberg about the treatment of human remains in museums. Academics and students were discussing questions including how human dignity is to be preserved by displaying human remains in respectful ways or not displaying them at all. While it has been a long-debated topic in archaeology and especially Egyptology concerning the [display of mummies](https://everydayorientalism.wordpress.com/2020/07/22/eotalks-your-mummies-their-ancestors-caring-for-and-about-ancient-egyptian-human-remains/) in European and North American museums, this topic seldom came into discussion in German academia, and I am delighted to see it took place in the form of a public exhibition free of charge. 

As a historian-archaeologist, most of my sources are inscriptions dedicated to the dead, people rich and poor, free and enslaved, all genders. Mostly, these inscriptions, with a handful of lines, are the only artefacts these dead people left to the world after thousands of years, rather than eye-catching figures like Augustus or Alexander. How to do justice to the evidence of existence for these dead people that left no records in historiography, while crucial in our understanding of human dignity, is a question seldom asked in the field of ancient history, partly due to the still [prevailing elitism in this field](https://www.routledge.com/A-Peoples-History-of-Classics-Class-and-Greco-Roman-Antiquity-in-Britain-and-Ireland-1689-to-1939/Hall-Stead/p/book/9780367432362), albeit some exceptional cases for [public writing](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/arts/classics/warwickclassicsnetwork/publicengagement/studentengagement/storiesofobjects/blog/rebecca/). Apparently, historians’ interpretations overwrite the possible space for multifaceted meaning-giving processes that could have taken place in antiquity. Still, amplifying voices through the fragmentary testimonies can reject the overall mainstream narrative about illusional histories. Funeral inscriptions and monuments, often alongside human ashes, were in antiquity made for display: there are even inscriptions on coffins trying to [have a dialogue with the passers-by](https://schnell-und-steiner.de/produkt/sprechende-objekte/). Should we then put the skeleton in such a coffin and, as they would have expected, have a dialogue with them? 

In my field in Greco-Roman antiquity, we are still struggling to let the wider audience acknowledge the vast majority of people, as commercialised representations like films and novels tell repeatedly stories of heroes or heavily sexualized/romanticized queens (I’m saying the popular *Odyssey*), hence reinforcing Eurocentrism and white supremacy (despite choices of some actors/actresses of Colour). Life of a manumitted businesswoman in Pompeii or an enslaved agricultural worker in Roman Palestine is no less valuable than an imperial soldier or an emperor: while such a statement may seem easy to make, there are still enormous difficulties even for ancient historians to acknowledge it, let alone our wider audience, be it our students or the public. 

How would our audience react if we added a funeral urn for a Roman child with an inscription and the ashes inside? And how would our curators’ team start a discussion? 

I happened to notice two hair queues from Qing-dynasty Guangzhou (not labeled clearly), imported by German merchants in 1911 from two revolutionaries. Unlike a case found in the Ulster Museum discussed by Elizabeth Crooke, who correctly criticised the neglect of the life history of the unnamed Chinese man whose braid must have been forcefully cut off, we have some information about how the merchants got the queues.[^14] They could have come from executed revolutionaries, or people who actively chose to cut off their queues to show resistance to the falling Qing regime and treat them as trash, or perhaps the note was made by German merchants who assumed what had happened. Whatever «educated» guesses we make, the two men whose hair queues remain in a box in Heidelberg had no chance to speak directly to us. The exhibition part «Debattenrunde» presented to the visitors a question: whether a real object must be displayed or whether such state violence can be presented with pictures, testimonies, etc. For me, a further question is: can braids only have one meaning? Could they also tell the story of Chinese emigrants concealing their origins by cutting their hair, or people who were forced to cut them under the threats of the Taiping Rebellion? What message would braids convey if they were displayed together with braids in other cultures? And after all, is it really possible to «tell the *full* story»? 

---

It is a long tour for me to travel back and to write down this piece: it is both a reconnection and a rehabilitation. It leaves more questions and answers for me and for you, my readers. How do we even begin to discuss what happened, hours, weeks, or years ago? Seven and a half years away has become a period of fermenting and eroding memories, leaving behind a mixture of emotions and all kinds of documents about all that has happened. Facing the braids cut off a century ago from my hometown or the colonial remnants of using human bones as decorations, can I really touch upon the existence of those people behind the «objects»? Am I carrying the evidence that they once existed, both in my memory and in my writing?

Nevertheless, at least those who are no longer living cannot be entirely excluded from our living society. After all, we will eventually join them.
